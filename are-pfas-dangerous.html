Are PFAS Dangerous to Humans?

By David Meldofsky, California-licensed attorney · Founder, Lawsuit Informer

Last updated: September 6, 2026

One of them, PFOA, is now classed by the World Health Organization’s cancer agency as causing cancer in humans. That is the same category as asbestos and benzene. What that ruling does and does not mean is the thing worth understanding, and it is not what most headlines suggested.

Key Takeaways:
  • In 2023 the WHO’s cancer agency classed PFOA as causing cancer in humans, and PFOS as possibly causing it.
  • That ruling is about whether a substance can cause cancer at all, not about how likely it is at everyday levels.
  • A study of a contaminated community found probable links to six conditions.
  • Later research has questioned some of those findings, and that argument is live.
  • The EPA now limits PFOA and PFOS in drinking water to 4 parts per trillion.

What the Cancer Agency Decided

In November 2023 the International Agency for Research on Cancer, part of the World Health Organization, reviewed two of these chemicals. It put PFOA in Group 1, meaning it causes cancer in humans, and PFOS in Group 2B, meaning it possibly does.

Group 1 is the category that holds asbestos, benzene and tobacco smoke. That comparison gets used to alarming effect and it is worth slowing down on, because the category does not mean what people assume.

What Group 1 Actually Means

The agency sorts substances by how confident it is that they can cause cancer. It does not sort them by how dangerous they are, or at what dose, or how likely you are to get ill.

So PFOA sitting in the same box as asbestos says the evidence that it can cause cancer is strong. It does not say that being around PFOA is as risky as breathing asbestos. Those are different questions, and the classification only answers the first.

The reasoning behind the decision reflects that. There was strong evidence in animals and strong evidence about how it behaves in human cells, but only limited evidence in people, drawn mainly from kidney and testicular cancer. Some scientists have argued the mechanistic evidence was read too generously. The classification is real and it is also contested.

The Community That Got Studied

Most of what is known about PFAS in people comes from one place. A chemical plant in West Virginia put PFOA into the drinking water of the surrounding area for years, and the class action that followed created something unusual: a science panel funded by the settlement and appointed by both sides.

The panel studied 3,713 workers and 28,541 other residents. In 2012 it reported probable links between PFOA and six conditions: kidney cancer, testicular cancer, thyroid disease, ulcerative colitis, high cholesterol, and high blood pressure in pregnancy.

The phrase probable link was not lawyer’s hedging. It was the standard the panel was required to apply under the settlement, and it means the panel judged a connection more likely than not on the evidence it had.

Where the Argument Is Now

That 2012 finding is more than a decade old, and the research has not stood still.

Some later work has questioned whether the kidney cancer and thyroid conclusions hold up against studies with better exposure measurement and better handling of other factors. Defence experts lean on that. Plaintiffs point out that the same period produced the cancer agency’s Group 1 decision.

Both things are true, which is the honest state of it. This is an active scientific argument rather than a settled question, and it is being fought inside the litigation as much as outside it.

Conditions the Research Keeps Returning To

  • Kidney cancer and testicular cancer
  • Thyroid disease
  • High cholesterol
  • Changes in liver enzymes
  • A weaker immune response, including to some vaccines
  • Ulcerative colitis
  • Pregnancy problems, including high blood pressure and lower birth weight

What the Regulators Have Done

In April 2024 the EPA set the first enforceable national limits for PFAS in drinking water. For PFOA and PFOS the limit is 4 parts per trillion each, which is among the lowest limits the agency has ever set for anything.

More telling is the number beside it. The EPA set the health-based goal for both at zero, on the view that no level of exposure is free of risk. The enforceable limit is 4 because that is roughly the lowest amount a laboratory can reliably detect, not because 4 is considered safe.

In 2025 the agency kept those two limits, rescinded the limits it had set for several other PFAS, and pushed the compliance deadline for water systems to 2031.

Linked To Is Not Caused By

A link means a condition shows up more often in a more exposed group. It does not show that the exposure produced any one person’s illness.

That gap matters twice. Medically, it is why a doctor will not tell you PFAS caused your cancer on the strength of a study. Legally, it is the harder half of a case, decided on which chemical, what your exposure was, when it happened, and what your records show.

How Much and How Long

Risk usually tracks the amount and the duration. PFAS complicate that because they build up rather than clearing out, so small amounts over many years is the pattern researchers worry about most.

Which chemical matters too. There are thousands of PFAS and nearly all the strong evidence involves two older ones, PFOA and PFOS. See How Long Do PFAS Stay in the Body? and How Do PFAS Get Into the Body?

Common Questions

Is PFOA officially a carcinogen?

Yes. The WHO’s cancer agency classified it as causing cancer in humans in 2023. That is a judgment about whether it can cause cancer, not about how likely it is at the levels most people encounter.

Which health conditions are linked to PFAS?

Kidney and testicular cancer, thyroid disease, high cholesterol, liver enzyme changes, a weaker immune response, ulcerative colitis, and pregnancy problems. Learn more →

Does PFAS exposure mean I will get sick?

No. A pattern across a population does not predict what happens to one person. How much, how long, which chemical, and your own health all matter.

How much PFAS in water is too much?

The EPA’s enforceable limit for PFOA and PFOS is 4 parts per trillion each. Its health-based goal for both is zero.
