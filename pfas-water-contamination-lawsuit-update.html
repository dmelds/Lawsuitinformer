Where PFAS Cases Stand Now

PFAS cases run on separate tracks that move on their own schedules. People who got sick bring injury claims. Water systems, towns and states bring claims for the cost of testing and cleaning up water. The companies being sued include 3M, DuPont, Chemours, Corteva and Tyco Fire Products.

Injury claims

On November 3, 2026, 3M agreed to pay $7.35 billion over nine years, through 2034, to settle PFAS injury claims. 3M puts the value in today’s dollars at $5.8 billion, and says between 115,000 and 120,000 people are eligible to join. The deal depends on enough people joining, and 3M can end it if too few do. 3M has published no payment amounts by illness.

On November 4, Judge Richard Gergel set November 13, 2026 as the date for lawyers to list every eligible claimant with the court. People without a lawyer have a separate way to be listed. A second order pauses the injury cases against 3M and sets strict deadlines for anyone who declines the deal. Full details are on AFFF Lawsuit Updates.

Claims against DuPont, Chemours and the other companies keep moving. The federal foam court in South Carolina had 15,270 pending cases in the Judicial Panel’s November report. No injury case has reached a jury, and the first test trial, set for October 2025, still has no new date. On November 20, both sides propose the next group of test cases, covering thyroid disease and ulcerative colitis.

On September 28 the judge dismissed roughly 13,000 claims over missing paperwork. Most can come back once the forms are filed.

If you already have a claim filed

If you are already in the litigation, right now is mostly paperwork. Check that your lawyer filed the required plaintiff fact sheet and records by the December 17, 2025 deadline. The court showed in late September that it will dismiss claims whose paperwork is missing, so confirm it rather than assume it.

Ask your lawyer to confirm your name goes on the court’s claimant list by November 13, and to explain the 3M settlement. To get an email when the court sets new trial dates, sign up for weekly PFAS updates.

If you have not filed yet, the questions are your exposure history, your diagnosis, and the filing deadline in your state. State deadlines for injury claims run from one to six years. Defense lawyers have argued that the EPA’s April 2024 drinking water rule and the public notices around it may have started the clock for many residents. Water systems and states

Before November 2026, the billion-dollar settlements in the news went to water providers and government bodies. The 3M and DuPont water funds together hold more than $13 billion for testing, treatment and cleanup. Carrier Global’s settlement of about $730 million, tied to the Kidde-Fenwal bankruptcy, is another. None of that money compensates a person diagnosed with cancer.

Four more water settlements, with Amerex, Archroma, Carrier and Nation Ford, got preliminary approval on September 10. Water systems can object until November 23, 2026 and opt out until December 22, and a fairness hearing is set for January 15, 2027. The 3M and DuPont treatment claim deadlines passed on July 31 and June 30, 2026.

States are settling their own cleanup claims. New Jersey’s deals with DuPont and 3M were approved in August 2026, and North Carolina settled with Chemours, DuPont and Corteva in September. New York sued the same companies in July over PFAS in consumer products. These cases resolve the government’s claims and leave residents’ own injury claims in place.

The national drinking water limits

A 2024 EPA rule set the first national PFAS limits for tap water, capping PFOA and PFOS at 4 parts per trillion each and limiting four other PFAS. Water industry groups challenged it, and the D.C. Circuit heard argument on September 18, 2026. EPA now plans to keep the PFOA and PFOS limits, give utilities more time, and drop the other four. The court has not ruled, and the rule stays in effect for now.

In August 2026, the same court upheld EPA’s listing of PFOA and PFOS as hazardous substances under the federal Superfund law. That listing decides who pays to clean up contaminated sites.

In Simple Terms

PFAS water contamination covers several different kinds of legal claim. They involve drinking water, groundwater, private wells, soil, or public water systems polluted by a family of chemicals people call forever chemicals, because they do not break down.

Some claims come from water providers and towns that want the cost of cleaning up their water. Others come from people who say years of PFAS exposure contributed to cancer, thyroid disease, ulcerative colitis, immune problems, or another serious illness.

Public Water Claims vs. Individual Injury Claims

Water system claims are brought by water providers, towns and government bodies. They ask for money to test for PFAS, install filters, treat the water, upgrade equipment and clean up.

Injury claims are different. They are brought by people who say PFAS exposure caused a serious illness. The exposure might have come from drinking water, firefighting foam, a workplace, a military base or somewhere else.

The difference matters because settlement news is easy to misread. A water system settlement says nothing about whether you have your own claim, whether your diagnosis is part of any case, or whether your exposure fits. For the evidence that may matter to an injury claim, see what evidence helps a lawsuit? For next steps, see what happens after you contact a lawyer?

How AFFF Firefighting Foam Fits In

AFFF is a firefighting foam used for decades on fuel fires, at airports, military bases, fire training grounds and industrial sites. Many AFFF products contained PFAS.

Water contamination claims and foam claims overlap because the foam soaked into the ground and reached groundwater, drinking water and soil nearby. That is why military bases, airports and fire training areas come up so often. The foam injury cases are gathered in one federal court in Charleston, South Carolina, known as MDL 2873, with Judge Richard Gergel presiding.

PFAS Settlements and What They May Not Cover

Most of the big PFAS settlements so far went to public water systems. 3M’s November 2026 injury deal is the first large one for people who got sick. The 3M and DuPont funds together hold more than $13 billion to help water providers pay for testing, treatment, filtration, and cleanup. State settlements cover cleanup and environmental damage tied to specific sites. New Jersey's deals with DuPont and 3M, approved in August 2026, are one example. North Carolina's September 2026 deal with Chemours, DuPont and Corteva is another.

A settlement headline rarely tells the whole story. A water system settlement covers claims by utilities and towns. A person who says PFAS made them ill has a separate claim. A state environmental settlement resolves the government's claims and usually leaves residents' own claims alone.

Before assuming a settlement applies to you, it is worth knowing who brought the claim, what it covered, and whether individual injury claims were dealt with separately. Usually they were not. For an overview of how class settlements work generally, see class actions; for state-by-state PFAS context, see PFAS in Drinking Water by State.

Why PFAS Lawsuits Can Take Years

These cases take years because so much is in dispute. Where the PFAS came from. How long it was there. Which company is responsible. Whether the warnings were good enough. Whether one person's exposure caused one person's illness.

Big PFAS cases are also grouped together before a single judge, so that evidence-gathering, expert fights and test trials happen once rather than thousands of times. That keeps the litigation organized, and it makes it feel slow if you are waiting. See mass torts and how long do lawsuits take? for more on these timelines.

The waiting is hard. You may already know PFAS turned up in your local water and still not know your exposure dates or what your medical records show. You may not know whether you qualify for anything, or how a claim of your own relates to the water system settlement.

Records That May Matter

Most people start by pulling together records about where they lived, where they worked, and their medical history. Which of those matter depends on the kind of claim.

One thing matters more than the rest: when you first learned about the exposure. Every state sets its own filing deadline, and in many of them the clock starts from that point rather than from when the exposure happened. For a state-by-state breakdown, see PFAS Lawsuit Statute of Limitations by State.

  • Addresses where the person lived during the possible exposure period
  • Private well or public water system information
  • Water notices, testing results, or local contamination reports
  • Military base, airport, industrial, landfill, or fire training site proximity
  • Firefighting, military, airport, industrial, or chemical-related work history
  • Medical records showing diagnosis and treatment history
  • Documents showing when the person learned of possible exposure or contamination

In Congress

Congress has three measures that bear on PFAS water cases: two bills and one amendment.

The 2027 defense bill, S. 4784, has gone furthest. It is the National Defense Authorization Act, the yearly bill that funds the military.

On July 14, 2026, a Senate vote to start debate on it failed, 50 to 46. A new motion to take it up was made on July 27, 2026. An amendment filed on June 24, 2026, S.Amdt. 6429, would add a liability shield for passive receivers, the water systems that take in PFAS they did not make. The Senate has not voted on the amendment.

The Water Systems PFAS Liability Protection Act, H.R. 1267, would protect water and wastewater utilities under the federal Superfund law. No one, including the federal government or a state, could collect PFAS cleanup costs or damages from them under that law. The shield covers PFAS a utility handled lawfully and ends where it acted with gross negligence or willful misconduct. The bill has sat with a House water resources subcommittee since February 12, 2025.

Both measures would change who can be made to pay for PFAS cleanup under federal law. None of them changes anyone’s claim unless both chambers pass the same text and the President signs it.

Common Questions People Ask

What is the current status of PFAS water contamination lawsuits?

On November 3, 2026, 3M agreed to settle its injury claims for $7.35 billion. On November 4 the judge paused injury cases against 3M. The Judicial Panel’s November 2026 report listed 15,270 cases pending in the federal firefighting foam litigation. A paperwork deadline for claimants passed in December 2025, and the first trial date was cancelled and has not been replaced. Separately, public water system settlements with 3M and DuPont continue to process Phase 2 claims, and additional state and class settlements have been announced.

Is there a PFAS settlement for individuals?

Yes, from 3M. On November 3, 2026, 3M agreed to pay $7.35 billion over nine years to settle PFAS injury claims. 3M says the deal covers about 99 percent of current claims, and it depends on enough people joining. Lawyers must list eligible clients with the court by November 13, 2026. Claims against DuPont, Chemours and the other companies remain in the stage before trial. The individual settlement status section above covers this in detail.

Are public water system claims the same as individual PFAS injury claims?

No. A public water system claim covers testing, treatment, filtration, infrastructure, and cleanup costs. An injury claim asks whether PFAS made one person sick.

Do PFAS water settlements end every possible claim?

Not necessarily. A water system deal and an injury claim can differ in who sues, what is owed, who is sued, and what rights get signed away.

How does AFFF firefighting foam relate to PFAS water contamination?

Some firefighting foam contained PFAS. Lawsuits allege that PFAS from that foam moved into groundwater, drinking water, soil, and nearby communities. The sites named most often are military bases, airports, fire training grounds, and industrial plants.

What deadlines should public water systems be aware of?

Under the 3M and DuPont water system settlements, Phase 2 systems generally faced a baseline testing deadline of January 1, 2026. The DuPont fund treatment deadline was June 30, 2026. The 3M fund treatment deadline was July 31, 2026. Water systems should confirm exact dates and requirements through the official settlement website and their own counsel.
