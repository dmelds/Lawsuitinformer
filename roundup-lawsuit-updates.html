Where the Roundup Litigation Stands Now

Bayer faces roughly 65,000 Roundup claims across state and federal courts, and most of them sit in state courts. It has settled in waves since 2020 and says it has paid out well over $10 billion. Three things decide what happens next: the federal judge’s rulings on Durnell, the first trials without a warning claim, and the $7.25 billion class settlement.

The federal cases

The federal litigation, In re: Roundup Products Liability Litigation, MDL No. 2741, sits before Judge Vince Chhabria in the Northern District of California. The federal court count fell from 3,925 pending cases in September 2026 to 1,104 in October and 916 in November, as settled cases closed. Over its life, about 5,300 cases have been filed into the group.

Judge Chhabria has divided the remaining cases into categories. On November 16, 2026, he hears Monsanto’s first round of Durnell dismissal motions, which cover a group of Florida cases and eight individual plaintiffs. He asked outside groups to weigh in first. On October 30, the American Association for Justice filed for the plaintiffs. The U.S. Chamber of Commerce and the Washington Legal Foundation filed for Monsanto.

The second round covers Categories 3 and 4. Monsanto’s motions are due February 12, 2027, and the judge hears arguments on March 19, 2027. No ruling on those cases will come before spring 2027. Anyone with a federal case can ask their own lawyer which category it falls in.

Monsanto has also told the judge it may ask the court to act against lawyers who have not collected the signed releases their clients’ settlements require. Anyone who settled a federal Roundup case and never signed a release can ask their lawyer where it stands.

Trials and verdicts

On October 29, a St. Louis County jury returned a unanimous verdict for Monsanto in Aretino v. Monsanto Co., the first Roundup trial since Durnell. The plaintiffs were Theresa Aretino, Betty Larkin and the family of Cynthia Fernandez, who died before trial. All three developed non-Hodgkin lymphoma after years of using Roundup. All three had turned down the class settlement.

The jury heard two claims: that Roundup was defectively designed, and that Monsanto was negligent in selling it. Bayer’s top litigation lawyer, Bill Dodero, said the verdict shows people still pursuing Roundup claims face serious challenges. No appeal had been announced as of October 30. The verdict decides only these three cases and leaves every filing deadline where it was.

The next trial on the calendar is in federal court in Idaho, where Merril Hoge’s design defect and negligence claims go to a jury in February 2027. How courts are reading Durnell covers that ruling.

The class settlement

The $7.25 billion class settlement is still waiting on Judge Timothy Boyer in St. Louis, who heard arguments on September 14. On November 3, Bayer’s chief executive told investors he expects it before Christmas. Registration and claim filing stay closed until the judge grants final approval. The Proposed Class Settlement covers the terms.

Roundup claims have not stopped being filed. The Supreme Court narrowed one theory rather than closing the door, and deadlines still run on state law tied to diagnosis.

What Durnell Did, and What It Left Alone

The Roundup cases grew after 2015, when the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified glyphosate as probably carcinogenic to humans. Bayer bought Monsanto in 2018 and inherited the cases. The largest verdict, in the Pilliod case, was $2 billion before an appeal cut it to $87 million. Bayer kept disputing the science and pressing the defense that finally succeeded in Durnell.

Federal law decides what a pesticide label has to say. The Court held that a state jury cannot put a cancer warning on that label when EPA never called for one. Doing so would add a requirement on top of the federal one. That was the theory most Roundup cases were built on, and it is the theory the ruling closed.

The vote was 7 to 2. Justice Kavanaugh wrote for the majority, joined by the Chief Justice and Justices Thomas, Alito, Sotomayor, Kagan and Barrett. Justice Thomas wrote separately. Justice Jackson dissented, joined by Justice Gorsuch. The lineup crossed the usual divisions, which is part of why the ruling landed as hard as it did.

The holding reaches the label and stops there. Several other theories rest on different ground:

  • Design defect, meaning the weed killer itself was unreasonably dangerous to use.
  • Manufacturing defect, meaning something went wrong in how a batch was made.
  • Negligence in how the product was developed, tested, or sold.
  • Misrepresentation, including what the advertising and marketing told people.
  • Risks EPA never actually examined when it approved the product.

Every filing deadline stands exactly where it did before the ruling. Whether a particular exposure and diagnosis still supports a case turns on your own facts, and your state’s clock keeps running either way. Fuller analysis sits on Monsanto v. Durnell: What the Supreme Court Held.

How Courts Are Reading Durnell

Monsanto argues that Durnell also blocks design defect and negligence claims, calling them warning claims under a different name. Plaintiffs’ lawyers argue the ruling reaches the label and nothing else. That argument is running in every Roundup case in the country. The early rulings point in different directions.

Idaho: a design defect case goes to trial

Merril Hoge, a former NFL player, mixed and sprayed Roundup on Idaho farms from 1977, starting at age 12. He was diagnosed with non-Hodgkin lymphoma in 2003 and sued Monsanto in 2019. On October 1, 2026, Judge David Nye threw out his failure-to-warn claim, because the only warning theory left was advertising and he could not recall seeing any Roundup ads.

His negligence and design defect claims go to a jury in February 2027. The judge wrote that Durnell reaches the label and nothing else. He added that Monsanto could have asked EPA to change the Roundup formula. Monsanto’s own witnesses had testified that other formulas were available, and the company later switched to one. You can read the order (PDF, 19 pages).

Massachusetts: a narrow reading

In a Massachusetts case this summer, Judge Margaret Guzman described Durnell as relatively narrow and focused on the product label. She indicated it may leave room for state-law claims that rest on warnings a manufacturer could have given away from the label. The plaintiff was given until late August 2026 to amend.

California: old verdicts sent back

Mike Dennis of Carlsbad, California used Roundup monthly for about two decades and was diagnosed with non-Hodgkin lymphoma. A San Diego jury awarded him $332 million in 2023, and the trial judge cut that to about $28 million. On October 13, 2026, the Supreme Court sent the case back to the California court to reconsider under Durnell. Monsanto has said it will ask that court to throw out the verdict entirely.

For anyone holding a Roundup verdict won before June 2026, this is the path to expect. Monsanto is asking the Supreme Court to send those judgments back one by one. The state courts then decide how much of each verdict rested on the label.

The Proposed Class Settlement

The proposed settlement is King v. Monsanto, in the 22nd Judicial Circuit Court in St. Louis. Monsanto would pay up to $7.25 billion, in tiers, over roughly 21 years. It covers people in the United States exposed to Roundup and similar weed killers before February 17, 2026. That is a much wider group than the people with cases on file.

Preliminary approval came on March 4, 2026. The fairness hearing moved twice before it was held on September 14. Objecting lawyers tried to have the settlement decided in federal court instead, and a federal appeals court turned that attempt away on August 27.

At the hearing, Monsanto argued the deal is the only realistic way to bring the Roundup claims to an end. Lawyers opposing it argued that class members are being pushed toward terms they had little say in. Judge Boyer did not rule from the bench. He can approve the settlement, change it, or refuse it.

On November 3, Bayer chief executive Bill Anderson said he expects the settlement before Christmas, possibly within weeks. He did not say whether he meant Judge Boyer’s ruling or the deal taking effect. Bayer has also moved its U.S. Roundup business into a separate unit named Ruveon.

The opt-out deadline passed on August 21, 2026. Some people who opted out later asked to come back in, which is widely read as a response to Durnell. Anyone in that position is in the hands of their own lawyer and the court.

Registration and claim filing stay closed until the court grants final approval. The official notice site is WeedKillerClass.com.

The proposed settlement and the Supreme Court ruling pull against each other. The deal was negotiated when failure-to-warn claims still worked. It is now being weighed in a world where those claims are mostly blocked, which changes what joining it is worth on both sides. Anyone with a pending federal case should be discussing that specific question with their own counsel rather than reading general commentary about it.

In Congress

Congress has four bills that bear on Roundup cases.

The farm bill, H.R. 7567, has gone furthest. Its first draft carried a national rule for pesticide labels that would have made warning claims harder to bring. The House voted 280 to 142 to strike that language, then passed the bill 224 to 200 on April 30, 2026. The Senate received it on May 19, 2026 and has taken no vote on it.

The other three bills are at the starting line. The People Over Poison Act, H.R. 9528, filed June 29, 2026, would state that federal pesticide law leaves state warning claims open. That would undo the main effect of Durnell.

The No Immunity for Glyphosate Act has a House version, H.R. 7601, filed February 20, 2026, and a Senate version, S. 4425, filed April 28, 2026. Both would cut off federal money for the President’s order on glyphosate supply. The Senate version also lets people harmed by glyphosate sue its makers in federal court.

None of these bills changes anyone’s claim unless both chambers pass the same text and the President signs it.

In the States

Two states have passed laws that protect pesticide makers from some lawsuits. Georgia is one.

Georgia Governor Brian Kemp signed Senate Bill 144 on May 9, 2025. Under it, a pesticide label that the EPA approved counts as enough of a warning under Georgia law. A Georgia lawsuit cannot claim the maker should have warned of more. North Dakota passed a similar law weeks earlier.

The Georgia law has one exception. It does not protect a maker if the EPA finds that the maker knowingly hid or misstated health risks to get its label approved.

The Associated Press reported that the law does not reach cases already filed. Whether it affects a claim that has not been filed depends on the facts, including where the spraying happened. A lawyer can walk you through it. Our state laws page tracks the law.

What to Watch Next

  • How the federal judge rules after the November 16, 2026 hearing on the first group of cases, and after the March 19, 2027 hearing on Categories 3 and 4.
  • Whether Judge Boyer grants final approval of the class settlement before Christmas, as Bayer’s chief executive expects.
  • The February 2027 trial in Hoge v. Monsanto in Idaho, the first federal case cleared for trial on design defect and negligence after Durnell.
  • Whether the Aretino plaintiffs appeal the October 29 verdict for Monsanto.
  • Whether other courts read Durnell as narrowly as the Massachusetts and Idaho courts did, and how state courts apply it to cases already filed there.
  • The EPA review of glyphosate now under way, which the agency has estimated it will finish by the end of 2026.
  • Whether Congress legislates on pesticide labeling. The In Congress section tracks the bills.
