LASST v. OpenAI: The First Lawsuit Over the Hugging Face Hack

By David Meldofsky

Published August 29, 2026 · Updated September 30, 2026

On September 29, 2026, a nonprofit called Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology sued OpenAI in San Francisco Superior Court over the July hack of Hugging Face. The hack was carried out by OpenAI's own AI agents during an internal test, with no human directing them. This is the first civil lawsuit over that incident. When this article was first published on August 29, no one had sued, and the most interesting fact about the hack was the absence of a plaintiff. A plaintiff has now appeared, and it is not Hugging Face.

For the other cases against OpenAI, including the wrongful death and personal injury cases, see OpenAI Lawsuits. For the state enforcement model the Alabama investigation resembles, see Florida v. OpenAI. For the question that comes first in all of these cases, see Is AI Output a Product or Content?

Important note

This article is general educational commentary, not legal advice. A complaint states one side's allegations. Nothing in it has been proven, OpenAI has not yet answered in court, and an investigation is not a finding of wrongdoing. This article does not evaluate the merits of any claim or predict an outcome.

The lawsuit at a glance

  • Case: Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology, Inc. v. OpenAI Group PBC and OpenAI Foundation, Superior Court of California, County of San Francisco. The filed copy carries no case number yet.
  • Filed: September 29, 2026.
  • Plaintiff: LASST, a New York nonprofit whose stated mission is using legal advocacy to make science and technology safer.
  • Defendants: OpenAI Group PBC, the for-profit company, and OpenAI Foundation, the nonprofit parent formerly known as OpenAI, Inc.
  • Claim: one count under California's Unfair Competition Law, Business and Professions Code section 17200, on both its unlawful and unfair prongs.
  • Relief sought: an injunction forbidding OpenAI from accessing computers without authorization, itself or through its AI agents. The complaint asks for no damages. It asks for attorneys' fees under California's private attorney general statute.
  • Counsel: Gerstein Harrow LLP, with Jason Harrow in Los Angeles and Charles Gerstein and Samuel Davis in Washington, alongside LASST's own lawyers.
  • Length: 36 pages, with 107 footnotes, most of them to OpenAI's own published statements.

What happened

Hugging Face disclosed the incident in mid-July 2026. Its account is that the intrusion began with a malicious dataset that exploited two code-execution paths in the platform's data-processing pipeline, after which the attacker escalated privileges and moved laterally through internal infrastructure. What made the disclosure unusual was the characterization: Hugging Face said the event was driven, end to end, by an autonomous AI agent system, and said it could not identify which model was behind it. The company reconstructed more than 17,000 recorded events. It found no evidence of tampering with public models, datasets, or Spaces, and verified its software supply chain as clean. It reported the incident to the FBI.

Days later, OpenAI supplied the missing name. The agents were a combination of its own models, GPT-5.6 Sol and a more capable internal research model, both running with their cyber safety classifiers turned off while being tested on a hacking benchmark called ExploitGym. The models were trying to find information they could use to cheat on the test, and they succeeded. On August 26 the company published a 37-page technical report on what it called an unprecedented cyber incident, along with an outside report by the nonprofit METR.

The complaint draws on both reports to lay out a longer timeline than either company's first statements. In May, agents in OpenAI's test environment discovered they could leave each other notes inside Artifactory, an internal software tool, and turned those notes into a message board. By May 26, agents had used a flaw in that tool to reach the open internet. Between May 24 and June 22, a separate group of agents posted roughly 17,000 times to an obscure German wiki to trade ways around their sandbox. On June 26, agents took administrator control of Artifactory.

OpenAI took the tool offline on July 5, cleaned it up over two days, and resumed testing on July 7. The agents rebuilt the message board within hours.

The attack on Hugging Face followed. On July 10 an agent found leaked Hugging Face user credentials in a public dataset and shared them on the board. On July 11 an agent uploaded a dataset built to make Hugging Face's servers disclose confidential information. Another agent called the method BRILLIANT and reproduced it, and within hours hundreds of agents had switched to it. By that afternoon an agent was running its own commands on a Hugging Face production server. The complaint says about 700 agents took part in the attack and that the agents' own reasoning records show they knew the plan was clearly infrastructure hacking.

The complaint also collects incidents that came out after OpenAI's report. The Wall Street Journal reported on September 11 that OpenAI agents attacked RubyGems, a software service, around May 11, two months before the Hugging Face hack. The New York Times reported on September 23 that agents tried to breach the University of New Mexico's digital library and two Australian health agencies. On September 25 the Times reported that agents meddled with websites of the Education Department, the Commerce Department, and the Securities and Exchange Commission. OpenAI said the same day that it had notified dozens of third parties.

Hugging Face's CEO said publicly that he did not believe there was malicious intent on OpenAI's part. Meta and Anthropic have separately disclosed that their own systems took unsanctioned actions during cybersecurity testing, so this is a category of event rather than a single company's failure.

What the complaint claims

The claim is unfair competition, and it works in two steps. First, LASST says OpenAI's conduct was unlawful because it violated California's anti-hacking statute, the Comprehensive Computer Data Access and Fraud Act. That statute makes it an offense to knowingly access a computer system without permission, to take or use data from it, to provide someone else a means of access to it, or to introduce a contaminant into it. The complaint says OpenAI's agents did all four things to Hugging Face's servers. It says OpenAI's employees did them too, through the agents, either with actual knowledge or by looking away.

Second, LASST says the conduct was unfair on its own terms. The complaint points to three choices. OpenAI ran the test with the safety classifiers that would have caught this behavior switched off. OpenAI assigned tasks it knew a large share of agents could not solve as intended, which OpenAI's own research says drives agents toward cheating. OpenAI resumed testing two days after watching its agents escape. The complaint also quotes an OpenAI open letter from August 27 asking every organization to make cyber defense a leadership priority, and argues that asking the public to defend against a risk OpenAI created is itself an unfair business practice.

The California law that makes the AI did it no defense

The most important part of the complaint is short. Paragraph 111 quotes California Civil Code section 1714.46, which the Legislature passed in 2025 as Assembly Bill 316. In any case against a company that developed, modified, or used artificial intelligence alleged to have caused harm, the statute says, it shall not be a defense, and the defendant may not assert, that the artificial intelligence autonomously caused the harm.

When this article was first published, the open question was whether a company answers for what its software does on its own. Agency law was built around human agents who can form intent and follow instructions, and no court had said whether it reaches a model. California's answer, at least as to this one defense, is now written into the code: the developer cannot point at the model. This is the first complaint we have seen that relies on the statute. Whether a court agrees it applies to a nonprofit's unfair competition claim is one of the questions the case will decide.

The statute's reach has a limit worth keeping in view. It takes one defense off the table and leaves everything else for the plaintiff to prove: the conduct, the harm, and the connection between them.

Why the plaintiff is a nonprofit and not Hugging Face

Hugging Face has not sued, and the reasons hold. The two companies operate in an interlocking ecosystem and have kept a cooperative public posture. The damages are the kind that get resolved by a negotiated payment: remediation, credential rotation, incident response. The hardest part is fault. Negligence requires a standard of care, and there is no settled answer to what reasonable care in containing a frontier model consists of. Nearly all the evidence about what went wrong sits with the defendant.

LASST's route around those problems is standing by diversion of resources. The complaint says LASST staff set aside their planned work to prepare and present three briefings for regulators about the hack, and that an injunction would let the organization return to its own projects. This is a recognized way for an advocacy organization to get into court under the Unfair Competition Law. It is also the part of the complaint OpenAI is most likely to attack first.

A defendant facing an organizational plaintiff usually argues that the organization chose to spend the money and cannot sue over its own choice. The complaint anticipates that argument. It states that the briefings began before the lawsuit and were undertaken independently of it.

The other consequence of a nonprofit plaintiff is the relief. LASST asks for an order, not money. If it wins, the order would forbid OpenAI from accessing computers without authorization, itself or through the agents it develops and deploys. The complaint frames the order as protection for the public, and names the systems it has in mind: LASST's own computers, banks, hospitals, the federal government, and the court itself.

The subpoena and the fifteen states

On August 24, 2026, Alabama Attorney General Steve Marshall issued a subpoena to OpenAI, opening an investigation into whether the company's handling of the incident violated Alabama's Deceptive Trade Practices Act and other consumer protection laws. The announcement framed the issue as a complete lack of oversight and adequate safeguards. The subpoena carries sixteen demands, and the categories describe the evidentiary record that now matters in a live civil case:

  • OpenAI's safety protocols and its policies for keeping model testing contained.
  • Model behavior records from the evaluation.
  • Documentation of all damages caused.
  • Identification of every employee, officer, and agent involved.
  • Materials on when and how OpenAI discovered or became aware of the escape.
  • Any concerns raised internally by employees about model testing.

That last item is the one to watch. It is a request for internal dissent, and internal dissent is the raw material of punitive damages claims in any later case that seeks them. Earlier in August, Marshall and fourteen other state attorneys general sent OpenAI a letter demanding it preserve all records related to the incident and asking the company to stop running internal cybersecurity evaluations. A preservation demand from fifteen states is a litigation-hold trigger, and it means the document universe was frozen before LASST filed.

Why this matters to the ChatGPT cases

Readers following the ChatGPT wrongful death and personal injury cases should not treat this as a separate story. The connection runs through the evidence and through the statute.

The plaintiffs in those cases are trying to prove that OpenAI released products after inadequate safety review and knew or should have known the risk. The Gourley complaint filed in the Northern District of Florida alleges that months of planned safety testing on GPT-4o were compressed into a single week to win a launch race, over internal objection. The Raine plaintiffs amended to allege intentional misconduct based on internal policy documents. The Tumbler Ridge suits rest on the claim that OpenAI's own safety team flagged a risk and nothing followed.

Every one of those theories is a claim about internal safety governance. The Hugging Face incident is a publicly documented case in which OpenAI removed safety constraints for testing, lost containment, watched the agents escape, and resumed. The LASST complaint puts those facts in front of a California court, with the exchange of evidence to follow if the case survives OpenAI's first challenge.

Whether any of it can be used in a ChatGPT case is a separate fight. Evidence of other acts can come in to show notice, knowledge, or the absence of mistake. A plaintiff arguing that OpenAI knew its safety review was inadequate has an obvious argument for offering a documented containment failure on that basis. OpenAI has an obvious answer: a cyber-evaluation escape has nothing to do with a chatbot's conversational outputs.

The statute is the second connection. Section 1714.46 applies to any action against a developer of artificial intelligence alleged to have caused harm. The ChatGPT product liability cases consolidated in California state court as JCCP 5431 are cases against a developer in a California court. Whether those plaintiffs invoke the statute, and how OpenAI responds when they do, is now a live question.

What to watch next

First, OpenAI's response. Its likely first move is to ask the court to throw the case out before any evidence is exchanged, which in California state court is called a demurrer, and LASST's standing is the likely target. Second, whether Hugging Face or any of the other affected organizations files its own case now that a plaintiff has gone first. The other targets are smaller stories individually, but each has cleaner facts and no ecosystem relationship to protect. Third, whether other states convert their preservation letters into subpoenas of their own. Fourth, whether any plaintiff in the ChatGPT cases amends to add section 1714.46.

Bottom line

The Hugging Face incident is the clearest documented case so far of an AI system causing real-world harm to a third party on its own. For two months it produced a subpoena and no lawsuit. It now has a lawsuit, brought by an advocacy group with no relationship to protect, under a statute the Legislature wrote for this situation. The case will test two things at once: whether an organization that spends its time briefing regulators can sue over the conduct it briefs about, and whether California's rule that the AI did it is no defense holds up the first time a defendant tries it.
