Pleural Mesothelioma: Symptoms, Causes, and Claims

By David Meldofsky, California-licensed attorney · Founder, Lawsuit Informer

Last updated: October 3, 2026

Pleural mesothelioma is the most common form of mesothelioma. It develops in the lining around the lungs and is strongly linked to past asbestos exposure. Because it affects the chest, its symptoms are often respiratory and easy to mistake for ordinary illness.

This page explains, in plain terms, what pleural mesothelioma is and how asbestos exposure is connected to it. It also covers the symptoms, and how a diagnosis can fit into a legal claim. This is educational background, not medical advice. Diagnosis and treatment belong with qualified medical professionals.

What Pleural Mesothelioma Is

Pleural mesothelioma develops in the pleura — the thin, two-layered membrane that lines the lungs and the inside of the chest wall. It is the most common type of mesothelioma, accounting for the majority of cases.

When asbestos fibers are inhaled, they can become lodged in this lining. Over many years they cause the cellular damage that may eventually develop into pleural mesothelioma. Because the disease sits around the lungs, it tends to interfere with breathing as it progresses. For the broader picture of the disease and its other forms, see the overview of what mesothelioma is.

How Common Pleural Mesothelioma Is

About 3,000 people are diagnosed with mesothelioma of all types in the United States each year, according to the American Cancer Society. Pleural mesothelioma makes up the large majority of them. Peritoneal mesothelioma, the next most common form, accounts for roughly 300 to 400 cases a year.

The average age at a pleural diagnosis is about 70. The disease is diagnosed in men far more often than in women, because the jobs that carried heavy asbestos exposure were held mostly by men.

How It Differs From Other Types and From Lung Cancer

Mesothelioma is categorized by where it begins. Pleural mesothelioma starts around the lungs and is the most common. Peritoneal mesothelioma starts in the abdomen and is the second most common, with rarer pericardial and testicular forms.

Pleural mesothelioma is frequently confused with lung cancer, because both affect the chest. They are distinct diseases in different tissues. Pleural mesothelioma forms in the lining around the lungs, while lung cancer forms in the lung tissue itself. Doctors establish that difference by testing, and it can matter for both treatment and legal claims, as explained in mesothelioma vs. lung cancer.

The Asbestos Link

Asbestos exposure is the primary and most well-established cause of pleural mesothelioma. Asbestos was used throughout much of the twentieth century in insulation, construction materials, and many industrial products. When those materials were cut, removed, or otherwise disturbed, microscopic fibers could be released into the air and inhaled. Because the fibers settle in the lining around the lungs, the pleura is the most common site for mesothelioma to develop. For where and how this exposure tended to happen, see asbestos exposure lawsuits and where asbestos exposure happened.

Who May Be at Risk

The people historically most at risk are those who worked around asbestos-containing materials, including:

  • Insulation workers and laggers
  • Shipyard workers and Navy veterans
  • Boiler, steam, and pipe workers
  • Construction and demolition workers
  • Power plant, refinery, and industrial maintenance workers

Family members could also be exposed secondhand — for example, through fibers carried home on work clothing — a route discussed in secondhand asbestos exposure. For occupation-specific background, see jobs with high risk of asbestos exposure.

Recognize one of those jobs as your own, or as a family member’s? That is the most useful thing to bring to a case review. You do not need records or product names to start. The places you worked, and roughly when, are enough.

Common Symptoms People May Notice

Because pleural mesothelioma affects the lining around the lungs, its symptoms are often respiratory and can include:

  • Shortness of breath, sometimes first noticed during activity
  • Chest pain, tightness, or pressure
  • A persistent dry cough
  • Fluid buildup around the lungs (pleural effusion), which a doctor may detect
  • Fatigue and unexplained weight loss

These overlap heavily with ordinary respiratory conditions, which is a major reason the disease is often diagnosed late. They are not a basis for self-diagnosis. For a fuller breakdown, see mesothelioma symptoms by type. Anyone with persistent or unexplained symptoms — especially with a known history of asbestos exposure — should see a physician.

How Long It Can Take to Appear

Pleural mesothelioma typically has a long latency period, often appearing 20 to 50 years after the asbestos exposure believed to have caused it. Someone exposed early in their working life may not be diagnosed until decades later. That long delay is part of why an older work history matters so much to understanding the disease. For more on the timing, see how long after asbestos exposure symptoms appear.

If pleural mesothelioma may be connected to past asbestos exposure, legal questions can follow the medical ones. A free, no-obligation case review can help clarify whether a diagnosis and exposure history may support a claim. No website can make that determination for you.

What a Pleural Mesothelioma Lawsuit Is

A pleural mesothelioma lawsuit is a personal injury case. The person with the diagnosis sues the companies responsible for the asbestos they breathed. The claim says the company knew asbestos caused cancer and sold or used it without warning the people who handled it. If the person has died, the estate and the family bring the same claim as a wrongful death case.

These are not class actions. One shipyard or plant will have exposed hundreds of people. Each worker breathed a different mix of products over a different span of years, so each case is tried on its own record.

Three kinds of companies are usually named. Manufacturers and sellers made the insulation, gaskets, packing, joint compound, and brake linings that carried the asbestos. Property owners ran the shipyards, refineries, power plants, and buildings where workers breathed it. Contractors installed and stripped the materials on those sites.

A claim against the person’s own employer usually goes through workers’ compensation instead. One case often names many defendants. A career in a shipyard or a plant meant contact with products from dozens of companies.

A case usually moves through these steps:

  1. A pathologist confirms pleural mesothelioma from a tissue sample, usually taken during a procedure to drain fluid from around the lung.
  2. A lawyer takes the diagnosis date and walks the person’s job history trade by trade, usually at no cost.
  3. The lawyer works out which products were on each job site in the years the person was there.
  4. Those products are sorted into companies still in business and companies whose trusts now pay claims.
  5. The suit is filed against the first group; trust claims go to the second.
  6. Coworkers and company witnesses answer questions under oath about what was used on site.
  7. Defendants drop out by settling as the case moves. Whoever is left goes to trial.

Because most people with this diagnosis are around 70, preference statutes matter more here than in almost any other kind of case. California lets a plaintiff who is over 70 and in poor health, or who is not expected to live six months, ask for trial within 120 days. For how a case moves from filing to resolution, see How Lawsuits Work.

What a Pleural Mesothelioma Case Can Recover

Pleural treatment runs to surgery, chemotherapy and radiation, often at a specialist center far from home. A case seeks the cost of all of it, along with the oxygen and home care that come later. It also seeks lost income and the pain the disease caused. A death case adds funeral costs and what the family lost in support and companionship.

Published averages are built from the handful of cases whose numbers became public, because settlements are almost always confidential. They predict nothing about one person’s case. What moves the number is how well the exposure can be pinned to named products, which companies are still solvent, and the person’s age and earnings at diagnosis. Asbestos settlement amounts works through how claims are valued.

Juries have returned large verdicts in shipyard and industrial cases. In September 2025 a Multnomah County jury in Portland awarded $34.2 million to Richard D. Long, who was diagnosed with pleural mesothelioma in 2023. Long had worked at the Dillingham ship repair yard on Swan Island from 1972 to 1985, handling asbestos gaskets and packing in the engine rooms of commercial vessels. The jury found the gasket maker John Crane liable, and $33 million of the award was for noneconomic damages.

That verdict came after a first trial ended in a mistrial earlier in 2025. Awards of that size are not the ordinary outcome, and courts often reduce them on appeal.

These cases are taken on contingency. The firm is paid a share of what it recovers, and nothing if it recovers nothing. The cost of investigating a decades-old work history falls on the firm rather than the client.

What the Law Generally Requires

State rules differ, but a pleural case is built from four things. Whether a particular history supplies them is what a case review is for.

  • The pathology. A report confirming pleural mesothelioma, and usually its cell type. Because almost nothing else causes this cancer, the diagnosis itself carries most of the argument about what caused it.
  • Where the fibers came from. The ships, plants, sites or job titles that put the person near airborne asbestos, or the household member who carried it home on work clothes. Few people can produce this from memory decades later.
  • Named products. The gaskets, packing, block insulation or cement that were actually in use where the person worked. Decades of litigation have built databases tying particular sites and years to particular brands.
  • Time left on the clock. Most states run the deadline from diagnosis, commonly one to three years, and a death claim from the date of death. The deadline is absolute, which is why the diagnosis date is the first question asked.

For a plain-language walk through the deadlines, see Is It Too Late to File an Asbestos Claim After Symptoms Appear Years Later?. For who the law treats as a potential claimant, see Who Qualifies for an Asbestos Lawsuit.

Not sure which products you were around? Most people with this diagnosis cannot name them from memory. Rebuilding that history from work, union, and military records is the first job of a case review.

Lawsuits and Asbestos Trust Claims

The insulation and packing makers that supplied shipyards and powerhouses were among the first to go bankrupt under these cases. By 2011 the Government Accountability Office counted about 100 asbestos bankruptcies and 60 trusts holding roughly $37 billion, and those trusts still pay.

A long shipyard or construction career usually crosses both groups, so most pleural cases run a court case and a stack of trust claims side by side. Each trust has its own forms, its own proof requirements and its own deadlines, separate from the lawsuit. See Asbestos Trust Funds and Claims for how those claims work.

What Information Matters in a Pleural Mesothelioma Case

A pleural case is reconstructed from paper, because the work in question happened thirty or fifty years ago. What carries the most weight:

  • The pathology report and medical records establishing the diagnosis
  • Employers, job sites and job titles, with the years at each, since the trade and the era point to the products
  • Union membership records, which often list job sites by year
  • Military service records, including ships and bases, for veterans
  • Names of products and brands the person remembers handling
  • Coworkers who can say what was cut, mixed or stripped alongside them, and family who saw the clothes come home

Common Questions About Pleural Mesothelioma

What is pleural mesothelioma?

It is the most common form of mesothelioma, developing in the pleura — the lining around the lungs and chest wall — and strongly associated with past asbestos exposure. Its symptoms are often respiratory.

How is it different from lung cancer?

Both affect the chest, but they are different diseases in different tissues. Pleural mesothelioma develops in the lining around the lungs; lung cancer develops in the lung tissue itself. The distinction is made by medical testing.

What are the symptoms?

Commonly: shortness of breath, chest pain or tightness, a persistent cough, fluid buildup around the lungs, fatigue, and unexplained weight loss. These resemble ordinary respiratory illness, so the disease is often found late. Only a physician can determine a cause.

How long after exposure does it appear?

Often 20 to 50 years, because of its long latency period. Many people are diagnosed long after they last worked around asbestos.

What is a pleural mesothelioma lawsuit?

It is a personal injury claim against the companies whose asbestos products the person breathed, usually on a job site. The claim is that those companies knew the dust caused cancer and sold the product without saying so. After a death, the family brings the same claim as a wrongful death case.

How much is a pleural mesothelioma case worth?

It depends on the facts: the diagnosis, the person’s age and income, the exposure evidence, and the companies involved. Most settlements are confidential, so published averages cover only the cases made public. In September 2025 a Portland jury awarded $34.2 million to a shipyard worker with pleural mesothelioma. Awards that large are not the ordinary outcome, and courts often reduce them on appeal.

Who can be sued in a pleural mesothelioma case?

Usually the companies that made or sold the asbestos products, the owners of the properties where the exposure happened, and the contractors who installed or removed the materials. A claim against the person’s own employer usually goes through workers’ compensation instead.

Is it too late to file if the exposure was decades ago?

Usually not. The clock in most states starts at diagnosis, not at the exposure, which is what makes a 1970s job still actionable. The window after diagnosis is short and varies by state.

What if I do not remember being exposed to asbestos?

Common, and not a barrier. Asbestos was rarely labeled, and a pipefitter or shipyard laborer handled dozens of products without ever being told what was in them. The history gets rebuilt from union books, service files, site records and coworkers.

Can a family file after a death?

Yes, as a wrongful death case brought by the estate or the family, on its own deadline measured from the date of death. Trust claims survive a death as well.
